## Challenge

Forecast electricity demand twelve months ahead for ten PJM load areas, well
enough that someone could plan capacity against it.

The difficulty isn't the trend. It's that demand has three overlapping
seasonalities — annual, weekly, and holiday — and the ten areas don't share
them. An industrial corridor and a residential suburb peak in different months
for different reasons, and the aggregate national curve hides both.

## Insight

One pooled model would have scored fine on the aggregate and been useless for
every individual decision.

The variance *between* areas was the actual finding. When ten regions peak at
different times, the interesting question stops being "how much demand is
coming" and becomes "where is it coming, and when" — which is a reallocation
question, not a forecasting one.

The second thing: the point forecast is the least useful output. A single number
gives a planner no way to size a buffer. The interval does, and an interval you
haven't validated for coverage is decoration.

## Approach

- **A Prophet model per load area.** Additive decomposition handles the
  multiple seasonalities cleanly, the changepoint logic copes with regime shifts
  without hand-tuning, and the whole thing stays interpretable enough to explain
  in a meeting.
- **Holiday and calendar regressors**, because the biggest single-day misses
  were all holidays. Cheap feature, large error reduction.
- **Rolling-origin backtests, not a random split.** Time-series data makes
  random cross-validation quietly fraudulent — you train on the future to
  predict the past and congratulate yourself. Rolling origin replicates how the
  model will actually be used, which is the only honest test.
- **Interval coverage checks.** An 80% interval that contains the truth 55% of
  the time isn't an 80% interval. I checked empirical coverage rather than
  trusting the label.

## Impact

Ten area-level forecasts with validated intervals and a per-area accuracy
figure, which together let a planner answer the practical question: *how much
do I commit, where, and how much do I keep in reserve.*

The honest part of the result is the error profile. The model was strong on
seasonal structure and weakest exactly where you'd expect — sharp
weather-driven peaks — and saying so is more useful than a single headline MAPE,
because it tells the planner which months to trust and which to hedge.

## What a marketer would do with this

**Forecast markets separately, then move the money.** If your ten regions peak
in different months, an evenly-spread annual budget is wrong everywhere at once.
Area-level forecasts turn media planning from a calendar exercise into an
allocation decision, and that's where the easy percentage points live.

**Plan against the interval.** Commit the lower bound, hold the gap to the upper
bound as flexible spend you can release when demand shows up. That's the
difference between a plan that survives a surprise and one that needs an
emergency meeting.

**Backtest the way you'll forecast.** Marketing teams evaluate forecasts the
same wrong way data scientists do — on a random holdout. Rolling origin is the
only validation that answers "would this have helped me last year," and it is
the number to put in front of a CFO.
