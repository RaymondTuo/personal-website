## Challenge

A wireless carrier was losing a meaningful slice of its subscriber base every
month. The standing response was a blanket retention offer: discount the people
who call in, discount anyone whose contract is ending, hope for the best.

That approach has a specific, expensive failure mode. Most of the discounts go
to customers who were never going to leave. You pay margin to retain people you
already had, and the campaign still *looks* successful because almost everyone
who got an offer stayed.

The question wasn't "who might churn." It was "who should we spend on."

## Insight

A churn model's job is not accuracy. It's ranking.

This matters because the honest ceiling on this dataset is a test AUC around
0.68 — respectable, not magic. If you treat that number as a verdict on the
model, you throw it away. If you treat it as a ranking tool, you find that the
top three deciles contain roughly 64% of everyone who leaves, in 30% of the
base. A retention team with a fixed call-center budget just got twice as
effective, using a model you'd be embarrassed to brag about.

The second half of the insight is where most churn projects stop too early:
risk is not the same as *persuadability*. Some of the highest-risk customers
have already made up their minds, and a discount to them is a discount to
someone walking out the door. The right next step is uplift modeling — ranking
by how much the offer *changes* behavior, not by how likely churn is. I built
the risk ranking first because it's the version you can ship this quarter.

## Approach

I worked up the model ladder deliberately, because the baseline is the thing
that tells you whether the complexity earned its keep.

- **Logistic regression first.** Interpretable, fast, and it sets the number
  everything else has to beat. It also surfaces the obvious drivers — tenure,
  overage history, handset age, support contacts — in a form a marketer can read.
- **Random forest, then XGBoost.** Gradient boosting took the AUC up a few
  points and, more usefully, picked up the interactions that matter: a price
  increase matters far more to a customer who has already called support twice.
- **Trained on AWS SageMaker** so hyperparameter search wasn't bounded by my
  laptop, with the artifacts versioned rather than living in a notebook.
- **Shipped as a Streamlit app**, which is the part that actually mattered. A
  retention manager enters a budget — "I can make 5,000 calls this month" — and
  gets back the call list, the expected churners captured, and the customers
  whose value doesn't justify the call.

A model behind an API that nobody on the marketing team can query is a model
that doesn't exist.

## Impact

The deliverable wasn't a score. It was a weekly list, ranked, with a budget dial
on top of it and a plain-English reason attached to each segment.

That reframing is what let a non-technical team use it: the output isn't
"customer 44812 has p = 0.71," it's "these 5,000 people are worth calling, and
here's what they have in common."

## What a marketer would do with this

**Tier the offer instead of flattening it.** Cross predicted risk with customer
value and you get four very different plays: high-risk/high-value gets a human
phone call and real money; high-risk/low-value gets a cheap automated save
attempt or gets let go; low-risk/high-value gets a loyalty touch and absolutely
no discount; low-risk/low-value gets nothing. Most retention budgets are
destroyed in that third box.

**Always hold out 10%.** Without a randomized holdout you cannot distinguish
"our campaign saved these customers" from "our model correctly found customers
who were going to stay anyway." This is the same discipline as
[incrementality testing](/work/project-geminae), applied to CRM.

**Use the drivers as creative input.** If handset age and support-contact count
dominate the model, that's not a modeling detail — it's telling you the offer
should be a device upgrade and a service apology, not a 10% discount.
