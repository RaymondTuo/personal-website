## Challenge

My CMU capstone team inherited a working seismicity prediction pipeline. It fit
the data well. The R² was good. The client was, reasonably, pleased with it.

And it could not answer their actual question, which was never "what will
happen" but "what happens *if we change this*."

That gap is the most common and most expensive failure in applied analytics. A
model can be excellent at describing the world it was trained on and completely
useless for deciding anything, and nothing in the standard metrics will warn
you.

## Insight

R² rewards a model for explaining the past. Decisions require a counterfactual.

Those are different mathematical objects, and a high score on the first tells
you almost nothing about the second. A variable can be the strongest predictor
in the model and have zero causal effect — it just happens to sit downstream of
something that does.

The practical consequence: before touching the model I had to establish what
the model was *for*, and define success in terms a decision-maker would
recognize. We replaced "maximize R²" with a short list of decision-relevant
metrics — calibration, performance in the tail that actually matters, and the
stability of effect estimates under stress.

## Approach

- **Draw the DAG before writing code.** Half the value was forcing an explicit
  statement of what we believed caused what. Confounders you haven't named are
  confounders you haven't controlled for, and a diagram makes disagreements
  visible in a meeting rather than buried in a regression.
- **Double machine learning for the estimate.** Orthogonalization lets flexible
  models handle the nuisance functions — all the messy nonlinear confounding —
  while keeping a clean, debiased estimate of the effect itself. You get ML
  flexibility without ML's tendency to launder correlation as insight.
- **Nonlinear enhancements to the existing pipeline**, so the comparison was
  fair: the causal estimate had to beat a strengthened baseline, not a
  strawman.
- **DoWhy refutation tests, as a gate.** Placebo treatment, random common
  cause, data-subset stability, unobserved-confounder sensitivity. An estimate
  that doesn't survive refutation doesn't get reported. This is the step that
  separates a causal *claim* from a causal *result*.
- **A dashboard connected to the pipeline**, because I was also the primary
  client liaison. Every number a stakeholder would be asked to act on needed a
  place where they could see it, interrogate it, and arrive at the meeting
  already informed.

## Impact

The headline finding was a correction, not a discovery: the effect the original
pipeline implied was roughly three and a half times larger than the effect that
survived orthogonalization and refutation.

Delivering that is awkward — you're telling a client their existing numbers
overstate things. It's also the single most valuable thing an analyst can do,
because every downstream decision sized against the inflated number was
mis-sized.

I ran client correspondence, scheduling, and the internal team's
communication alongside the modeling, which meant the finding arrived as a
conversation we'd been building toward rather than a surprise in a final
deck.

## What a marketer would do with this

This project *is* marketing attribution with the labels swapped.

**Last-click is the raw correlation bar.** It's the top bar in that chart:
technically accurate about what it measures, and systematically overstating the
channel that happens to sit closest to the conversion. Every channel claiming
the same sale is a confounding problem, not a reporting problem.

**Geo holdouts and PSA tests are the DAG.** When you can randomize, randomize —
a geo-split holdout settles in three weeks what six months of MMM debate won't.
When you can't randomize, Double ML on observational data is the honest
fallback, and it belongs next to the MMM rather than instead of it.

**Make refutation a reporting standard.** Before anyone presents an
incrementality number, it should have survived a placebo: run the same analysis
on a period or audience where the campaign didn't run. If it still shows lift,
the number is measuring your methodology, not your marketing.
