## Challenge

Allocate scarce advanced-node and packaging capacity across products with
different margins, different volumes, and different strategic weight.

This is the canonical constrained-resource problem. Demand exceeds supply, every
product line has a credible claim, and the usual resolution is proportional
fairness — give everyone a percentage of what they asked for. It feels
equitable and it is reliably the worst available answer, because it ignores
which products are actually constrained by which input.

## Insight

The allocation is not the interesting output. The shadow prices are.

Solving the LP tells you how to split today's capacity. The dual values tell you
something much more actionable: what one additional unit of each constrained
resource is worth. In this formulation only three constraints bind at all, and
the top one is worth roughly nine times the third.

That reorders the entire strategy. Two of the five constraints have slack —
meaning every dollar and every hour of engineering attention spent relieving
them returns precisely nothing. Most organizations spend heavily on whichever
bottleneck is most visible rather than the one that's binding, and an LP's dual
values are the cheapest possible correction to that instinct.

## Approach

- **Formulate explicitly.** Decision variables as units allocated per product,
  objective as total contribution margin, constraints as capacity at each stage
  of the process. Writing the model down is itself a deliverable — it forces
  every stakeholder's implicit assumption into a single line of algebra where
  it can be argued about.
- **Solve, then immediately interrogate.** The optimal objective value is the
  least interesting number the solver returns. Duals, slacks, and reduced costs
  are where the decisions are.
- **Sensitivity analysis over point answers.** How far can a margin assumption
  move before the allocation changes? If the optimum flips on a 2% change in an
  input you estimated by hand, the real finding is "go measure that input."
- **Reported as a decision brief, not a solver log.** Three binding
  constraints, their marginal values, and a ranked list of what to buy next.

## Impact

A defensible allocation, and a ranked shopping list of bottlenecks with a dollar
value attached to each.

The sensitivity work was what made it credible. Any allocation model can produce
a confident-looking table; being able to say "this answer holds unless margins
move more than *x*" is what lets someone commit to it.

## What a marketer would do with this

**Identify the binding constraint first.** Every growth team has one — creative
production, sales capacity, inventory, approvals, or a channel that's already
saturated. Pouring budget past a binding constraint doesn't buy growth, it buys
queue. And effort spent on a constraint with slack is pure waste, no matter how
loudly it's complained about.

**Translate shadow prices into marketing language.** The marginal value of
one more unit of capacity is the marginal value of one more creative
variant, one more SDR, or one more unit of stock. It converts
"we need more resources" into "the next unit of this is worth $42k and the next
unit of that is worth $5k."

**Refuse proportional fairness.** Giving every channel or region a flat share of
a constrained budget guarantees you underfund the ones where the next dollar
compounds. Fairness is a people problem; allocation is a math problem. Solve
them separately.
