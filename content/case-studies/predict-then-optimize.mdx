## Challenge

Build something that doesn't just predict which assets will do well, but decides
how much of a finite budget goes into each one.

The distinction sounds academic until you've watched a team do the first half
and stop. A ranked list of predicted winners is not an allocation. The moment
you have constraints — minimums, maximums, risk tolerance, how much you're
willing to change from last period — the ranking stops being enough, and
"just put the money in the top one" is a strategy that works until it
spectacularly doesn't.

## Insight

Prediction accuracy and decision quality are different objectives, and
optimizing the first does not reliably improve the second.

A slightly worse model inside a well-constrained optimizer beats a better model
with no constraints, almost every time. The reason is error asymmetry: the
optimizer is not equally sensitive to every mistake. Being wrong about an asset
you were going to hold at its floor anyway costs nothing. Being wrong about the
one the optimizer wants to overweight costs everything. So the model should be
evaluated on the errors that move the allocation — which is a different loss
function than the one it was trained on.

That also means the two stages have to stay separate. Merging them is tempting
and it destroys your ability to debug: when the allocation looks wrong you need
to know whether the forecast was bad or the constraints were.

## Approach

- **Stage one: classifiers predict direction and probability.** Calibrated
  probabilities, not raw scores — the optimizer consumes these as expected
  values, so a miscalibrated 0.8 does real damage downstream.
- **Stage two: a quadratic program in Gurobi.** Maximize expected return minus a
  risk penalty built from the covariance matrix. The quadratic term is what
  stops the optimizer from piling into correlated bets that look independent on
  a spreadsheet.
- **Constraints as the expression of strategy.** Per-asset floors and ceilings,
  total budget, and a turnover limit so the allocation can't whipsaw every
  period. These aren't technical details; they're where judgment enters the
  model, and writing them down forces that judgment to be explicit.
- **Compared against an equal-weight baseline** throughout, because any
  allocation method that can't beat 1/N is an expensive way to be average.

## Impact

A working two-stage pipeline where the forecast and the decision are separately
inspectable, which is what makes it trustworthy enough to act on.

The more durable result was learning to read the optimizer's dual values. When a
constraint binds, the shadow price tells you what relaxing it is worth — which
converts "can I have more budget?" from an argument into a number.

## What a marketer would do with this

**This is media-mix optimization.** Swap assets for channels, expected return
for predicted incremental revenue, and the covariance matrix for channel
overlap, and the formulation is unchanged. Most budget allocation happens in a
meeting where the loudest channel owner wins; this is the alternative.

**Encode the real constraints.** Brand needs a floor regardless of
last-click performance. Paid search has a ceiling where it stops being
incremental and starts harvesting demand you already own. Nobody can
triple a channel in one quarter — that's a turnover limit. Write those down and
the optimizer stops producing answers your team would never execute.

**Use the shadow prices to argue for budget.** The dual on your total-budget
constraint is the marginal return on the next dollar. That is a far stronger
case to a CFO than a deck full of channel ROAS, because it answers the only
question they're asking: what does the *next* dollar buy.
